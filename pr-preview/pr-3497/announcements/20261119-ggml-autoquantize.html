Sub-4-Bit AutoQuantize with GGML Formats: Matching a QAD Release Without Training#

Author:

Model Optimizer Team

Date:

November 19, 2026

Tags:

autoquantize, quantization, ggml, mixed-precision, gptq

GGML’s IQ formats store an MoE expert weight in 1.75 to 2.56 bits, and the experts hold most of an MoE’s bytes. That makes them the place to spend a tight budget. A uniform assignment gives every expert the same format, but some layers need more bits than others. AutoQuantize picks a format per layer under a size budget, but below 4 bits its default search did no better than uniform IQ2. We changed how it scores and calibrates candidates, and published the result as two AutoQuantize recipes that run with one hf_ptq.py command.

On NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 Bolt 74B-A9B, post-training quantization with the recipe matches the 24GB release at the same size. That release was trained with Quantization-Aware Distillation (QAD); the recipe needs no training.

Highlights#

  • QAD-release accuracy from PTQ alone. At the release’s 2.0625 bits per expert weight (22.4 GB of language-model weights), the recipe’s held-out KL to BF16 is 0.076, against the release’s 0.071: within one standard error. It scores AA-LCR 60.1, GPQA 79.0 and SciCode 41.9, against the release’s 59.3, 76.0 and 40.2.

  • 26% lower KL than uniform IQ2_XXS at the same size, and 7.8 ± 1.4 points more on AA-LCR.

  • Scoring in a low-bit context matters most. The default search scores each layer with the others in BF16. At the same budget its model has 94% higher KL and is 14 points lower on AA-LCR, 5 on GPQA and 6 on SciCode.

  • GPTQ matters next. Rounding the same format assignment with imatrix alone, without GPTQ, raises KL 17% and costs 3.8 ± 1.5 points on AA-LCR.

  • Lowest KL on Qwen3.6-35B-A3B too, by a smaller margin. At 2.75 effective bits, the recipe’s held-out KL is 0.100, against 0.105 for the default search and about 0.106 for uniform IQ2. These margins are within one standard error.

  • One command, two runs. The same hf_ptq.py --recipe command searches, then finalizes and exports. Recipes are available for Nemotron 3.5 Bolt and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B.

NVFP4 and FP8 are weight-only here (W4A16 and W8A16), like the IQ formats, because the budget counts weight bits.

AutoQuantize scores how much each candidate format hurts each layer, then solves for the lowest-scoring assignment under the budget (see AutoQuantize: A Fast Automatic Mixed-Precision Assignment). By default a layer is scored with every other layer in BF16. At NVFP4 and FP8 budgets that works, because one layer’s error stays small next to the others’. At 2 bits it does not. On Bolt, each routed-expert group’s IQ1_M error looks small on its own, so the default search put every routed expert at IQ1_M and spent the bytes on the shared experts, at 14-16 bits. The routed experts’ errors compounded, and the model came out worse than uniform IQ2_XXS at the same size: held-out KL 0.146 against 0.103.

What the Recipes Change#

Three changes, all set in the recipe YAML:

  1. Score in a low-bit context. With auto_quantize_method: kl_div and method_options: {context_quant_level: k}, each candidate is scored while the other searched layers sit at the format k steps above their lowest-bit candidate (0 is the lowest). The score then includes how a layer’s error compounds with the others’, so the scores add up closer to the model that ships. Bolt uses context_quant_level: 1 (IQ2_XXS, the second-lowest candidate). Qwen3.6 uses lowest, also IQ2_XXS. With a context, AutoQuantize picks formats with a linear program, as the gradient method does.

  2. Calibrate with GPTQ, twice. During the search, each IQ candidate is calibrated with GPTQ and imatrix on BF16 inputs (local GPTQ), so a layer’s score does not depend on the formats picked for the layers before it. Once the formats are fixed, finalize_algorithm reruns GPTQ and imatrix sequentially, each layer on its quantized predecessors.

  3. Search the experts, fix the rest. The experts hold about 90% of Bolt’s linear weights. The recipe searches only the routed and shared experts and keeps every other layer at the release’s format: W4A16 NVFP4 for the Mamba in_proj and out_proj, and Q8_0 for attention, the MoE latent projections and lm_head.

The search part of the Bolt recipe:

auto_quantize:
  constraints:
    effective_bits: 2.0625
  module_search_spaces:
    - module_name_patterns: ["*mixer.experts*", "*mixer.shared_experts.*"]
      candidate_formats:     # IQ1_M, IQ2_XXS, IQ2_XS, IQ2_S with local GPTQ + imatrix,
        ...                  # then W4A16 NVFP4 and W8A16 FP8
      allow_no_quant: true   # BF16 is a candidate too
    ...                      # the other layers: one fixed format each
  auto_quantize_method: kl_div
  method_options: {context_quant_level: 1}
  finalize_algorithm:
    - method: gptq
      layerwise: {enable: true}
      ...
    - method: imatrix

Results on Nemotron 3.5 Bolt#

Nemotron 3.5 Bolt is a 74B-parameter hybrid Mamba-Transformer MoE with 9B active parameters. The 24GB release keeps its experts at IQ2_XXS, the Mamba in_proj and out_proj at W4A16 NVFP4, and attention, the MoE latent projections and lm_head at Q8_0, and was then trained with QAD.

The search scored candidates on 128 of 512 calibration documents (up to 512 tokens each) from Nemotron post-training data. Held-out KL is measured against BF16 on 128 other documents (62.2k tokens). The 2.25-, 2.5- and 2.75-bit rows reuse the 2.0625-bit search’s scores, solved at their budgets. Every row except BF16 keeps the release’s formats outside the experts.

Held-out KL has a standard error of 0.002-0.008. AA-LCR is pass@1 over 16 seeds with a 65,536-token generation budget, GPQA Diamond is averaged over 32 repeats and SciCode subtask accuracy over 8. Between two rows, paired by question, differences resolve to about ±1.5 points on AA-LCR, ±0.7 on GPQA and ±1 on SciCode. Our harness scores BF16 and the release lower than the model card does on AA-LCR and GPQA, so compare rows within this table only. Every quantized row was evaluated on an exact BF16 dequantization of its quantized weights, so the table measures accuracy, not speed.

At the release’s size, PTQ with uniform IQ2_XXS experts loses 7 AA-LCR points to the release. The recipe recovers them with the same bytes and is 3 points higher on GPQA. Most of the remaining AA-LCR gap to BF16 is runaway reasoning, not wrong answers: a generation that hits the token budget scores as wrong, and those are repetition loops. Among finished generations, every model answers about 70% correctly, BF16 included, but at 2.25 bits (24.0 GB) the recipe’s model hits the budget in 15.9% of generations against BF16’s 7.6%. A 512-token KL objective does not see that, so adding bytes moves AA-LCR less than it moves KL.

The recipe’s 22.4 GB model uses IQ1_M. Its search picks each expert format from IQ1_M, IQ2_XXS, IQ2_XS, IQ2_S, W4A16 NVFP4, W8A16 FP8 and BF16, and it keeps the routed experts of 8 of the 40 MoE layers at IQ1_M, 31 at IQ2_XXS and one at IQ2_XS: 2.01 bits on average. The bits that saves go to the shared experts, which hold under 3% of the expert weights; they average 3.9 bits, mostly W4A16 NVFP4 and IQ2_S.

These numbers come from a script with the recipe’s search and finalize settings. The script loads the checkpoint with its own weight renaming and calls mtq.auto_quantize. One setting differs from the recipe: the script’s scoring context held the Mamba conv1d at FP8, while the recipe keeps it in BF16. Both finalize it in BF16.

  • Context. The best scoring context is the model that ships: 2-bit experts with the other layers at their final formats. Erring toward BF16 for the non-expert layers costs little; scoring them at 2 bits, below their final formats, costs 23% in KL and 5.6 AA-LCR points. Scoring with the other experts in BF16 is the costliest choice, even with the linear program.

  • Calibration. Without GPTQ, the same assignment loses about as much KL as going from 2.0625 to 2.25 bits (22.4 to 24.0 GB) gains.

  • Scope. Searching the non-expert layers too lost at every size we tried, 6-17% in KL and 1-2 GPQA points. A layer type with a well-tested format is better fixed than searched.

Try It#

Run the command once to search, then run it again to finalize and export. Both runs share the --auto_quantize_checkpoint file:

python examples/hf_ptq/hf_ptq.py \
    --pyt_ckpt_path nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Bolt-74B-A9B-BF16 \
    --trust_remote_code \
    --recipe models/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Bolt-74B-A9B-BF16/auto_quantize/iq1_iq2_gptq_nvfp4_fp8_experts_at_2p0625bits \
    --auto_quantize_checkpoint <search.pth> \
    --export_path <output-path>

For Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, use --pyt_ckpt_path Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and --recipe models/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B/auto_quantize/iq2_gptq_nvfp4_fp8_experts_at_2p75bits.

  • Other budgets. Change constraints.effective_bits in a copy of the recipe. The Bolt table’s 2.25- to 2.75-bit rows are the same search solved at those budgets.

  • One GPU or several. hf_ptq.py holds Bolt on one GPU: on a GB200, a search with two expert candidates peaked at 184 GB and the finalize at 193 GB. To score the candidate formats in parallel, run one format shard per GPU and merge them, as described in Scoring and finalizing AutoQuantize in separate runs.

  • Bolt loads through its own code, hence --trust_remote_code; it was tested with transformers 5.18.

  • Serving. The export is a Hugging Face checkpoint with packed GGML blocks. For serving GGML checkpoints on vLLM, see vLLM with GGML recipes.

Each recipe’s README lists its candidates and results: Nemotron 3.5 Bolt and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. For writing your own, the sub-4-bit AutoQuantize guide covers the same settings.
