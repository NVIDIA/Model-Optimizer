Evaluating Models Efficiently During Optimization Experiments#

Author:

Daniel Korzekwa, Rohan Joshi, Chad Voegele, Chenjie Luo

Date:

November 18, 2026

Tags:

evaluation, docs, quantization, qad, fast-experimentation

Introduction#

When you optimize a reasoning model with NVIDIA Model Optimizer, how do you verify improvements without overlooking regressions? Average accuracy can hide doomed reasoning loops (repetitive reasoning that continues until the output-token limit is reached), missing answers, inappropriate guessing, or excessive output length.

Because model generation is stochastic, we repeat evaluations to measure how scores vary between runs. You may assume that reliable evaluation requires many repetitions. But could repeating by default waste time? How many questions and repetitions do you need to measure improvements and rule out regressions?

This article helps you evaluate models quickly and reliably during optimization experiments. We illustrate these guidelines using real evaluation results from two NVFP4 Qwen3.6-35B-A3B checkpoints.

For standardized evaluation and acceptance checks before releasing optimized models, follow the ModelOpt evaluation skill and ModelOpt results-comparison skill. For benchmark commands, see the LLM evaluation examples.

What should you measure, and how?

  • Show where the model improves or worsens: use paired statistical tests to distinguish model differences from evaluation noise.

  • Check for regressions: use confidence intervals and non-inferiority tests to rule out unacceptable regression, even if the baseline is not shown to be better.

  • Assess model reliability: measure doomed reasoning loops, missing answers, and whether the model abstains appropriately.

  • Measure inference efficiency: compare output length, including reasoning tokens.

How can you evaluate faster?

  • Choose between question-level and repetition-level analysis: match the approach to your goal and evaluation budget.

  • Collect as much evaluation data as you need: use pilot results to choose enough questions or repetitions to assess regression.

  • Test known weaknesses on a focused subset: failure-prone questions can reveal targeted improvements with fewer responses; check broader performance separately.

What should you measure, and how?#

We compare two NVFP4 Qwen3.6-35B-A3B checkpoints. Baseline underwent post-training quantization (PTQ) and quantization-aware distillation (QAD), preserving accuracy but increasing doomed reasoning loops. Candidate was trained directly from Baseline using Kahneman–Tversky Optimization (KTO), which learns from desirable and undesirable responses, aiming to reduce those loops while preserving accuracy. We use MMLU-Pro, SciCode and AA-Omniscience to illustrate these evaluation guidelines. See the reproducibility notes.

Choose a primary improvement metric before inspecting results. Prefer Candidate if it improves that metric and meets your non-inferiority criteria for the others. Regressions highlight areas to improve.

Show where the model improves or worsens#

We first average each model’s correctness across repetitions of each question, then compute Candidate − Baseline for each question. We calculate t-based 95% confidence intervals across these paired question-level differences. Changes are reported in percentage points (pp), not relative percentages. For simplicity, this example treats questions and SciCode subtasks as independent.

We use paired question-level tests here to compare models across the domain represented by the benchmark. Pairing can reduce variation caused by question difficulty. For standardized reporting on a fixed benchmark, ModelOpt uses unpaired Welch t-tests on independent, complete-run scores to estimate uncertainty from variation between runs of those exact questions.

Our primary question is whether Candidate is better than Baseline. A one-sided paired t-test can detect improvement with less data than a two-sided test for any difference. Reverse-direction p-values are reported for context.

Choose test directions before inspecting results. We use p < 0.05 for each test, without a stricter threshold for testing multiple metrics. Testing more metrics gives noise more opportunities to appear as an improvement.

“Yes” means evidence of higher Candidate accuracy (p < 0.05); “no evidence” means higher accuracy was not established.

Check for regressions#

No evidence of a difference does not establish no regression. Choose an acceptable accuracy-loss margin, then use a non-inferiority test to assess whether the loss is smaller. The checks below use one-sided paired t-tests. “Yes” means the stated accuracy loss is ruled out (p < 0.05); “no evidence” means it has not been ruled out.

Assess model reliability#

Average accuracy alone does not show whether a model behaves reliably: also measure doomed reasoning loops, missing answers, and whether it abstains when appropriate.

Measure

Recommendation

Doomed reasoning loops

Use cap-hit rates to screen for potential loops, then inspect the text to confirm repetition. A cap hit reaches the configured response-token limit (131,072 here).

Missing final answers

Report how often responses contain no final answer.

Abstention ratio

Count an abstention when the final response is non-empty and the judge grades it NOT_ATTEMPTED using AA-Omniscience’s rubric. Report abstentions / (all responses − correct responses) alongside accuracy. The denominator includes abstentions, incorrect and partial answers, and missing answers. AA-Omniscience is an example benchmark.

Rare failures can make t-based approximations unreliable, so we use paired-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals and one-sided paired permutation tests for the rates below.

How can you evaluate faster?#

When reducing question coverage, choose a representative subset before inspecting results and use the same question IDs for both models. See Data preparation for how we selected the benchmark questions.

Choose between question-level and repetition-level analysis#

Both approaches are valid. Choose based on your goal and the balance between precision and evaluation speed:

  • Repetition-level: estimate accuracy on a fixed benchmark. Treat its question set as a black box: compute one average score per complete run, then estimate uncertainty across independent runs. Multiple complete passes can be expensive.

  • Question-level: get accurate results quickly for the domain represented by a benchmark. Use representative questions: compute an average score per question across repetitions, then estimate uncertainty across questions. With sufficiently independent questions, this approach can support faster experimentation.

Both approaches produce a single aggregate score per checkpoint for comparisons across experiments. With the same number of repetitions per question, they give the same mean score; they differ in how uncertainty is estimated. Keep per-question results for more detailed paired comparisons across experiments.

Is question-level analysis reliable on real benchmarks compared with repetition-level analysis? We compare the two approaches by spending the same response budget on more unique questions or more repetitions per question, then measuring how much the resulting scores vary. Each allocation produces 20 observed scores, using 1,000 responses per model for MMLU-Pro and 280 for SciCode.

At the same response budget, more questions with fewer repetitions give more consistent Candidate accuracy estimates on both MMLU-Pro and SciCode, with lower observed standard deviation (SD). Shading shows ±1 SD across 20 group scores; solid lines show their mean.

For Candidate − Baseline accuracy differences, more repetitions give slightly lower observed SD on MMLU-Pro, while more question coverage gives lower SD on SciCode.

For SciCode, more subtasks with fewer repetitions give a more consistent estimate of Candidate’s cap-hit-rate reduction relative to Baseline, with lower observed SD.

Collect as much evaluation data as you need#

You may not need the full evaluation budget to detect an improvement or rule out an unacceptable regression. The data needed depends on the model pair, metric, acceptable regression margin, and statistical target.

How many responses show fewer cap hits on SciCode?

After completing all 20 repetitions, we analyzed subsets of the saved responses: first 10–294 subtasks with one repetition, then additional repetitions on all 294 subtasks. In this retrospective comparison, the p-value first fell below 0.05 at 588 responses per model and below 0.001 at 882.

How many responses rule out a 1 pp accuracy loss on MMLU-Pro?

We first compare subsets of 50–1,000 questions with one repetition, keeping approximately the same subject proportions, then add repetitions on all 1,000 questions. To rule out a 1 pp accuracy loss, the non-inferiority test first reached p < 0.05 with one repetition on 750 questions. It first reached p < 0.01 at 3,000 responses and p < 0.001 at 12,000 per model among the tested sizes.

Freeze a subset from earlier baseline failures to screen for targeted improvements. Our focused subset uses 11 held-out subtasks × 10 repetitions (110 responses per model). For comparison, the SciCode row shows the broader evaluation: 294 subtasks × 20 repetitions (5,880 responses per model).
